Understanding WHO-GMP Certification: What Bulk Buyers Should Verify

Almost every pharmaceutical supplier will tell you their products are "WHO-GMP certified." For a procurement or quality team placing a bulk order, that phrase should be a starting point for questions, not the end of them. Here's what the certification actually covers, what it doesn't, and the specific documents worth asking for.

What WHO-GMP Actually Certifies

WHO Good Manufacturing Practice guidelines set requirements for the facility, equipment, personnel training, documentation and quality-control systems used to manufacture a pharmaceutical product. In India, WHO-GMP compliance is assessed by the State Licensing Authority in coordination with CDSCO, and a certificate is typically valid for three years from the date of issue.

A WHO-GMP certificate from an Indian manufacturer carries real weight in international procurement, particularly across Africa, Southeast Asia, Latin America and the Middle East. But read the scope carefully: it certifies the process and the environment, not any individual batch. That distinction shapes everything you should ask for next.

Schedule M: India's Own GMP Baseline — And Why It Just Got Stricter

Alongside WHO-GMP, every pharmaceutical manufacturer in India must comply with Schedule M of the Drugs and Cosmetics Act, 1940, enforced by the Central Drugs Standard Control Organisation (CDSCO). Schedule M is not optional and not export-specific — it is the domestic floor.

That floor was raised substantially. The Government of India notified a revised Schedule M on 28 December 2023, significantly expanding GMP requirements for plant, equipment and quality systems. The compliance timeline was staged by manufacturer size:

  • Large manufacturers (turnover above ₹250 crore) had to comply from 28 June 2024
  • Small and medium manufacturers were given a longer runway, subsequently extended to 31 December 2025 for those who applied to CDSCO under notification G.S.R. 127(E) dated 11 February 2025

The upgrades the revision demands are substantial and capital-intensive: cleanroom qualification, HVAC validation, purified water and Water for Injection system qualification, computerised system validation, equipment qualification (IQ/OQ/PQ), stability storage capacity, and a documentation overhaul.

For a buyer, this is useful signal. A manufacturing partner that has already completed its revised Schedule M upgrade has demonstrably invested in exactly the systems that keep sterile injectable quality consistent from batch to batch. It is a fair and specific question to ask.

COPP: The Document Export Buyers Actually Need

If your order crosses a border, WHO-GMP alone often isn't the document your importing authority wants. The Certificate of Pharmaceutical Product (COPP) is issued by the national regulatory authority of the exporting country in the WHO-recommended format, and it confirms three things at once: that the product is approved for sale in the country of manufacture, that it is manufactured in a GMP-compliant facility, and that it meets quality standards for export.

Two practical points: a COPP is product-specific, not facility-wide, and it is typically valid for around two years — shorter than the WHO-GMP certificate it sits alongside. In India both are filed through the CDSCO online portal, so a legitimate supplier can retrieve them without difficulty.

What To Actually Request From a Supplier

  • A copy of the current WHO-GMP certificate, including its issuing authority, scope and validity period
  • The specific manufacturing license covering the dosage form and molecule you're ordering — a facility's WHO-GMP status doesn't automatically extend to every product category, and sterile injectables are a category of their own
  • Confirmation of revised Schedule M compliance status for the manufacturing site
  • A batch-specific Certificate of Analysis (CoA) for the lot you're actually receiving
  • A product-specific COPP where the order is for export or import registration
  • Independent test reports, where the supplier operates third-party testing in addition to in-house QC

Why Independent Testing Still Matters

A manufacturing certification tells you the process was built correctly. It doesn't independently confirm that a specific batch met specification — that's a separate, testing-side question. This is precisely why pairing WHO-GMP certified manufacturing with independent, third-party testing closes a gap that manufacturing certification alone leaves open. One checkpoint validates the process; the other validates the output.

A Simple Red Flag

If a supplier is reluctant to share certificate copies, license numbers or batch CoAs before you've placed an order, treat that as information. Documentation should be part of the sales conversation, not something you have to fight for after the fact.

Two-Step Quality Assurance, Explained

At VRSN Pharma, every formulation we supply is built on two separate, verifiable checkpoints — not one. We call it our Two-Step Quality Assurance Approach, and it's worth explaining why we structured it this way rather than relying on a single certification, as much of the market does.

Step One: WHO-GMP Certified Manufacturing

Our products are manufactured through WHO-GMP certified manufacturing partners, following established manufacturing and quality-control practices. This step controls everything that has to be right before a single vial is filled: facility design and cleanroom classification, HVAC and water systems, equipment qualification, personnel training, batch documentation, and in-process quality control.

For sterile injectables specifically, this is where the hardest engineering sits. A lyophilised powder for injection has no margin for a contaminated fill environment or an unqualified water system — which is why we treat a partner's certification scope, and its revised Schedule M readiness, as a selection criterion rather than a formality.

Step Two: Independent Quality Testing

Finished products are then subjected to relevant quality testing through an independent NABL-accredited laboratory, wherever applicable, to verify specified quality parameters.

The word "independent" is doing real work in that sentence. NABL — the National Accreditation Board for Testing and Calibration Laboratories — is India's national accreditation body, and it assesses laboratories against ISO/IEC 17025:2017, the international standard for testing and calibration competence. Accreditation is not a one-time badge: NABL assessors verify compliance clause by clause, and laboratories are reassessed periodically to keep it.

Two consequences matter for a buyer:

  • An accredited laboratory's report reflects independently assessed technical competence, traceability and measurement reliability — not a supplier's own assertion about its own product
  • Reports issued under the NABL accreditation symbol are recognised under the ILAC Mutual Recognition Arrangement in over 100 countries, and NABL accreditation is recognised by CDSCO, BIS, FSSAI and other Indian regulatory bodies

Testing conducted by the same organisation that manufactured the product answers a narrower question than testing conducted by an accredited laboratory with no commercial stake in the result.

Why Both Steps, Rather Than Either One

Manufacturing certification alone tells a buyer that a facility is capable of producing a compliant product. It doesn't independently confirm that a specific batch actually did. Independent testing alone, without certified manufacturing behind it, verifies an output without any assurance that the process producing it is controlled and repeatable.

Run together, they answer two different questions — was this built right? and did this batch come out right? — and neither answer substitutes for the other.

What This Means On Your Purchase Order

In practical terms, a buyer working with us can ask for, and expect to receive, the manufacturing partner's WHO-GMP certificate and license scope, a batch-specific Certificate of Analysis, and independent test documentation where applicable — before committing to a bulk quantity. We also dispatch samples ahead of bulk commitment so your own QC team can run its checks independently of ours.

That's the confidence we're aiming to build with every formulation we add to the catalog — across VRTOPAN™, VRCEFXONE™ and VRCEFXONE™-SB today, and carried forward as our product range grows.

Ceftriaxone Injection IP: A Quick Reference for Procurement Teams

Note: This is a procurement and handling reference, not clinical or prescribing guidance. For dosing, administration and clinical decisions, refer to the approved package insert for the specific product and to your clinical team.

Ceftriaxone is one of the more widely stocked injectable antibiotics in institutional formularies worldwide. If it's moving through your purchase orders, here's the background that tends to matter on the procurement side — form, packaging, storage and the questions worth putting to a supplier.

What It Is

Ceftriaxone is a third-generation cephalosporin antibiotic, typically supplied as Ceftriaxone Sodium IP — a sterile powder reconstituted before administration rather than a ready-to-use liquid. Its broad-spectrum profile is a large part of why it remains a formulary staple across hospitals and institutional buyers.

Why the Sulbactam Combination Exists

You'll frequently see ceftriaxone offered both on its own and combined with sulbactam. The reason is resistance.

Some bacteria produce beta-lactamase enzymes that break down beta-lactam antibiotics — ceftriaxone among them — before the drug can act. Sulbactam is a beta-lactamase inhibitor: it irreversibly inhibits those enzymes, protecting the ceftriaxone molecule and extending its useful spectrum against beta-lactamase-producing strains. Sulbactam contributes little antibacterial activity of its own; its job is to keep the partner antibiotic viable.

In India, the ceftriaxone + sulbactam combination has been approved by the Drug Controller General of India (DCGI) for use against ESBL-producing Gram-negative organisms. Given the scale of antimicrobial resistance pressure in Indian institutional settings, many formularies now stock both presentations deliberately rather than treating the combination as a straight substitute.

In our catalog that maps to VRCEFXONE™ (Ceftriaxone 1000 mg per vial) and VRCEFXONE™-SB (Ceftriaxone & Sulbactam 1.5 gm per vial).

Form & Packaging Considerations

  • Supplied as a sterile powder for injection (IV/IM use) — not a ready-to-use liquid, which changes storage, handling and on-ward preparation requirements
  • Commonly packaged as single vials, multi-vial cartons, or configured to bulk carton sizes for institutional orders
  • Strength is specified per vial — 1000 mg (1 g) for the standalone formulation, 1.5 gm for the ceftriaxone & sulbactam combination
  • Many presentations ship with a matched diluent ampoule (Sterile Water for Injection IP or Sodium Chloride Injection IP) in the same carton — confirm whether your quoted price includes it

Storage & Handling

As a sterile powder, ceftriaxone generally has more forgiving storage requirements than liquid formulations. Typical label conditions are storage at controlled room temperature — commonly stated as below 25 °C or 20–25 °C depending on the market — protected from light, with an unopened shelf life in the region of three years.

Two handling points that are worth flagging to receiving and pharmacy teams, because they are frequently missed:

  • Post-reconstitution stability is short. Published product characteristics commonly demonstrate in-use chemical and physical stability of around 24 hours at 25 °C, or up to four days at 2–8 °C. Reconstituted vials are not indefinitely storable.
  • Calcium-containing diluents are incompatible. Ringer's and Hartmann's solutions should not be used to reconstitute or further dilute ceftriaxone, as particulate formation can result.

Exact conditions vary by manufacturer and market, so always confirm against the approved package insert for the specific product you are buying rather than a general reference like this one.

What To Ask a Supplier

  • Confirmation of Indian Pharmacopoeia (IP) compliance for the specific strength and pack size you need
  • A batch-specific Certificate of Analysis for the lot being supplied
  • The manufacturing partner's WHO-GMP status for sterile injectables specifically, not just for the facility in general
  • Remaining shelf life at the point of dispatch — not just the total shelf life from manufacture
  • Whether the diluent ampoule is included in the pack and in the quoted price
  • Realistic lead time from order confirmation to dispatch at your required quantity
